1a. The Positioning Statement

Your current positioning is a category description, not a position. "LinkedIn growth strategist helping coaches and founders" tells a prospect nothing they couldn't read on 50 other profiles. No enemy, no mechanism, no specificity. Here is the corrected version:

The SIGNL Positioning Statement
"I help SaaS and agency founders escape the follower treadmill and turn LinkedIn into a predictable inbound pipeline — using a done-for-you content and positioning system built around one thing: pipeline, not vanity metrics."
Why this works

Enemy named: "follower treadmill" and "vanity metrics" — what your ICP has already paid for and resented. Outcome named: "predictable inbound pipeline" — not followers, not impressions. Pipeline. Mechanism named: "done-for-you content and positioning system" — separates you from coaches and course sellers.

Why SaaS + agency founders, not coaches

Nick FarrenKopf is a SaaS founder. He is your only verifiable proof point. Targeting SaaS and agency founders means you can point to him and say "this person looks exactly like you." Build credibility in the niche your case study lives in first. Expand later.

5a. What drove your 100K+ posts

You hit 100K+ impressions with hot-take / contrarian opinion posts. This tells you something specific: your audience responds to you being willing to say what others won't. The LinkedIn algorithm rewards content that creates a strong reaction — agreement or disagreement, not neutrality. Generic advice disappears. A strong contrarian take gets shared, debated, and remembered.

The replication principle

Every viral post you write will have the same DNA: a claim that makes someone in your ICP say either "yes, EXACTLY" or "wait, that's wrong." Both reactions cause engagement. Neutral posts cause scrolling. Your natural writing style is your biggest asset — don't sand it down to be palatable. The founders who will hire you need to see the edge first.

5b. The Nick FarrenKopf Case Study Post — Framework

This is your most important piece of content. It needs to be structured to generate inbound DMs, not just impressions. Do not write it as a brag. Write it as a diagnostic — "here's why his LinkedIn wasn't working, here's what we changed, here's what happened."

1
The hook — 3 options to test
Option A: "I took a SaaS founder from 0 to 3,490 LinkedIn followers in 90 days. Here's the exact problem I fixed first." Option B: "Most SaaS founders have the same LinkedIn problem. Here's how I diagnosed it — and what happened after." Option C: "Nick had a great product and zero LinkedIn presence. 90 days later: 3,490 followers, 400K impressions. Thread on what we actually did."
2
The before — make it specific and painful
Describe exactly what his LinkedIn looked like before. Not "it wasn't performing." Name the specific problems: the headline, the About section, the content frequency, the positioning gap. Make the reader recognise themselves.
3
The diagnosis — name your mechanism
"Here's what I found when I ran the Signal Audit on his profile..." Walk through 2–3 specific things that were broken. This is the section where you demonstrate expertise. Be specific about what was wrong and why it was wrong.
4
The intervention — what you changed
Describe the 3 biggest changes made. Headline rewrite. Content pillar definition. Engagement strategy. Be specific about what changed and why. This is the trust-builder section.
5
The result — verifiable, specific, understated
"90 days later: 0 to 3,490 followers. 400K total impressions. His profile is live — you can check it right now." Do not exaggerate. The verifiability is the credibility. Point to his profile explicitly.
6
The CTA — one specific ask
"If your LinkedIn looks like Nick's did at the start of this — I run a full diagnostic called the Signal Audit. $350, 5 days, personal Loom. DM me 'AUDIT' and I'll tell you if it's the right fit."
Engagement strategy — the part most people skip

Post 3 times per week. Leave 10 genuine comments per day on posts from people in your ICP. Not "great post." Real comments: add a perspective, challenge an assumption, share a relevant experience. Do this every single day. This is how you become a familiar name before you send a cold DM. It is also how LinkedIn's algorithm starts showing your content to more people in your niche.

SECTION 08

Risks &
Failure Modes

The three most likely ways SIGNL fails in 90 days. Early signals. Interventions. Pivots.

RISK 01The Preparation Trap — Building instead of selling
What it looks like

Day 15 arrives and the Notion CRM is beautifully organised, the freebie PDF has been redesigned 3 times, the landing page is almost done, and zero DMs have been sent. This is your most documented pattern. It happened with Buraq. It happened with every previous job. Infrastructure feels like progress.

Early warning signal

If by Day 7 you have not sent your first DM, you are in this trap. The signal is: you are doing tasks that feel productive but don't require speaking to a human.

Intervention

The rule is simple: before any design work, any refinement, any infrastructure build — send 5 DMs first. Every single day. DMs before everything. The freebie can be ugly. The landing page can be basic. The first DM just needs to be sent.

RISK 02The Credibility Spiral — Prospect asks hard questions and you fold
What it looks like

A prospect asks "how many clients have you worked with?" or "can you show me more case studies?" and instead of going straight to Nick's results, you apologise, over-explain, discount the offer, or let the conversation die. The credibility challenge is the second biggest conversion killer after not reaching out at all.

Early warning signal

You re-read your DM before sending it and add a disclaimer about your experience level. Or on a call, you volunteer that Nick is your only case study before they've even asked.

Intervention

Memorise this and say it out loud until it sounds natural: "My job is to grow your LinkedIn, not mine. Here's what I generated for Nick FarrenKopf — 0 to 3,490 followers, 400K impressions, 90 days. His profile is live right now, you can check it." Then redirect to their problem. You never apologise for your follower count. Ever.

RISK 03The Metric Inflation Problem — Case study falls apart under scrutiny
What it looks like

Your operating manual flags this directly: "A case study with inflated metrics that could destroy credibility with any serious prospect who checks." If any part of the Nick case study cannot be verified by a stranger looking at his live profile, it is a liability. Not an asset.

Early warning signal

You feel hesitation before sending the case study link. Or you qualify it with "approximately" or "around" when describing results.

Intervention

Before any outreach: verify exactly what his profile shows right now. Use only the numbers that are visible and checkable by anyone. The correct statement is: "0 to 3,490 followers, 400K total impressions over 90 days — his profile is live, you can check it right now." If the follower count has changed since then, adjust. Use whatever the verifiable reality is. Credibility built on one clean case study is worth more than ten inflated ones.

8c. Pivot Options — If LinkedIn growth stalls

If by Day 60 you have sent 300+ DMs and closed zero clients, the problem is one of three things: wrong ICP, wrong offer framing, or wrong channel. Before pivoting the whole business, test each variable individually. But if you need to pivot the service:
